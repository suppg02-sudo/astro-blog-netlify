# Could Sharing a Video Land You in a Police Station? The Alarming Case That Should Worry Us All

What happens when simply asking a question online becomes grounds for a police interview? If you think it couldn't happen to you, think again. A recent case involving former MP Andrew Bridgen has exposed a deeply troubling reality about freedom of expression in the digital age—one that could affect absolutely anyone who posts, shares, or comments on social media.

## The Incident That Should Make You Pause

Here's the situation: Andrew Bridgen shared a video on Twitter. Let me be clear—he didn't create this video. He didn't film it, edit it, or produce it. It was a publicly available AI-generated video featuring a fictional character called "Amelia." His commentary? A simple question: "Please watch and share if you think she has a point."

For that act alone—for merely inviting discussion about content that was already circulating publicly—he found himself sitting in a police station undergoing an interview under caution.

The video in question featured an AI-generated female character making statements that some might find controversial. The police allegation claimed the content "would be deemed grossly offensive to a reasonable person." But here's where it gets particularly ironic: the character "Amelia" was originally created by the Home Office itself as part of a game designed to identify children who might be at risk of radicalisation. The video Bridgen shared was essentially satirising a government-created character.

## Voluntary Interviews: Not as Innocent as They Sound

Now, you might be thinking, "But it was just a voluntary interview—surely that's different from being arrested?"

This is a dangerous misconception that far too many people hold.

A voluntary interview carries exactly the same weight as an interview following an arrest. You're still being interviewed under caution. Everything you say is still admissible in court. The consequences are just as serious. The only real difference is that you're not technically in custody—but make no mistake, the legal implications are identical.

If you refuse to answer questions during such an interview, the court can potentially draw what's called an "adverse inference" against you. This means a magistrate or jury might view your silence as suspicious. However, as Bridgen's case demonstrates, there are strategic ways to handle this situation.

## The Power of a Prepared Statement

Bridgen's approach to the interview offers a masterclass in protecting yourself during police questioning. Rather than answering questions spontaneously—which carries the risk of your words being misinterpreted or taken out of context—he submitted a prepared statement and then answered "no comment" to every subsequent question.

His statement began with a clear explanation for this approach: "I am making this prepared statement voluntarily. I do not trust the police to interview me fairly in this matter because of my previous experiences... and I'm concerned that any spontaneous answers I give could be misinterpreted and then used against me unfairly."

This is crucial. By documenting his reason for not answering questions, he provided his legal representatives with ammunition to counter any negative inferences that might be drawn from his silence. It's a technique that serves to protect your rights whilst still cooperating with the process.

## When Did "Grossly Offensive" Become So Subjective?

The central allegation against Bridgen hinged on whether the video he shared was "grossly offensive to a reasonable person." This is the threshold that must be met for certain communications offences.

But here's the problem: the definition of "grossly offensive" seems to have expanded dramatically in recent years. Content that might make some people uncomfortable, or that some might disagree with, is being treated as criminal material. The bar has been set so low that merely asking whether someone "has a point" about a controversial topic can trigger a police investigation.

Many people might find certain content objectionable. They might not like it. They might even find parts of it offensive. But "grossly offensive" is supposed to be a much higher bar—it's meant to capture truly reprehensible material, not merely controversial opinions or satirical commentary.

## The Bigger Picture: Why This Matters

This case touches on something fundamental about the relationship between citizens, the state, and freedom of expression. When a former Member of Parliament can be hauled into a police station for asking a question about a video—particularly one featuring a character created by a government department—what does that say about the state of free speech?

We live in an era where sharing the wrong content, asking the wrong question, or inviting the wrong kind of discussion can result in a criminal investigation. The police eventually took no further action in Bridgen's case, but the message has been sent: be careful what you share, and be even more careful what questions you ask.

## Protecting Yourself in the Digital Age

So what lessons can we take from this case?

**First**, understand that anything you share online could potentially be scrutinised. Even content you didn't create. Even content that's publicly available. Even when you're simply asking a question rather than making a statement.

**Second**, if you ever find yourself called in for a voluntary police interview, treat it with the same seriousness as an arrest. Seek legal advice immediately. Consider whether a prepared statement might be appropriate for your situation.

**Third**, know your rights. Freedom of expression remains a valid defence in many of these cases—but only if you understand how to assert it properly.

## The Bottom Line

The fact that this case was eventually dropped doesn't make it any less concerning. The mere existence of such investigations has a chilling effect on free speech. People become afraid to share, afraid to question, afraid to engage in legitimate debate.

In a healthy democracy, we should be able to ask questions without fear of police intervention. We should be able to share content and invite discussion without worrying whether we'll be sitting in an interview room explaining ourselves.

The case of Andrew Bridgen serves as a stark reminder that these freedoms cannot be taken for granted. Stay informed, know your rights, and think carefully about how you engage online—not because you've done anything wrong, but because the boundaries of acceptable discourse seem to be shifting in troubling ways.
