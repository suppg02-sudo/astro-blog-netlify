A video landed on my radar last week arguing that only five structural positions survive AI commoditization: trust, context, distribution, taste, and liability. Nate B Jones calls them "the five durable verticals." The thesis is clean — AI commoditizes production, and what remains valuable are the layers production cannot replace. What caught my attention wasn't the argument itself (which is directionally right) but what happens when you map it against a system that's been building exactly those layers without realizing it. My evolution project — a meta-system governing how an AI infrastructure improves itself across 8 domains — turns out to be accidentally building moats in four of the five verticals. Here's what that mapping reveals about what to build next, and what to stop building.

## The Question

The evolution project runs a unified Capture → Analyse → Improve → Reuse → Monitor loop across 8 domains: prompts, menus, skills, triggers, decisions, attention, intent, and roadmap. It has 9 factories at full DNA alignment, 400+ tracked evolution artefacts, a knowledge compiler pipeline (raw → compiled → wiki), and cross-domain bridges that connect improvements in one area to dependent systems in another. The question is whether this is infrastructure that AI will eventually make redundant, or whether it's structural in the way Jones describes — something the models can't replicate because it compounds over time in ways that can't be extracted from training data.

## The Evidence

Mapping each vertical against the evolution project reveals four strong alignments and one critical gap.

**Context (95% alignment).** Jones argues that context owners — Notion, Salesforce, Epic — become the internet's choke point because agents without context are just chatbots. The evolution project has accumulated 2,846+ vector memories, a structured research knowledge store (eRAG), and a knowledge compiler that processes raw inputs into verified wiki articles. Every captured prompt, every menu signal, every decision, every skill improvement cycle adds data gravity. The 8-domain artefact table with quality scoring, human ratings, and version chains is building the permissioning layer Jones identifies as the most valuable real estate in the AI economy. Nobody else has this specific accumulation because nobody else has been running this specific loop on this specific stack for this long.

**Taste (85% alignment).** Jones calls taste "orchestration quality — curated agent experiences designed by domain experts." The evolution engine's quality scoring (0-10) across all 8 domains is taste codification — converting human judgment into machine-actionable data. The menu factory signal tracking is taste measurement. The approval gates are taste enforcement. The cross-domain bridges (schema_to_prompts, menus_to_skills, intent_to_prompts) create emergent intelligence that no single domain produces alone. This is the most abstract moat but potentially the most defensible: nobody can replicate the accumulated signal data without running the same loops for the same duration.

**Trust (40% alignment).** Jones identifies trust as the verification layer for agentic commerce. The evolution engine has trust infrastructure — quality scores, approval gates, version chains, audit trails — but it isn't productized as a trust signal. An artefact with quality score 8/10, human approval, and 47 uses at 92% effectiveness is a trust claim analogous to "Powered by Stripe," but right now that claim is invisible outside the system.

**Liability (55% alignment).** Jones notes that audit trails and governance boundaries become moats because "the AI did it" doesn't survive court. The evolution engine captures creation timestamps, parent chains, use counts, and metadata for every artefact — the raw material for accountability. But it tracks quality scores, not outcomes. It doesn't log whether an improved prompt actually produced better results, or whether an optimized menu led users astray.

**Distribution (60% alignment) — the critical gap.** Jones argues that when supply is infinite, curation becomes the scarcest resource. The evolution engine improves things internally but doesn't solve discovery. There's no mechanism for external agents to find and use the accumulated intelligence. The 60+ skills are a capability catalog, but there's no marketplace, no queryable API, no agent-to-agent discovery layer.

## The Counter-Arguments

Three things could undermine this mapping.

**The over-engineering thesis.** A recent analysis of Claude Code creator Boris Cherny's workflow argued that this system may be over-engineered relative to what moves the needle — Cherny achieves similar outcomes with 5 parallel agents and a single markdown file. If simplicity beats sophistication, the 8-domain evolution engine is a liability, not an asset. But the moats framework suggests the opposite: the over-engineering IS the moat, because it's taste infrastructure that can't be trivially replicated. The question isn't whether a simpler system works today. It's whether a simpler system compounds the same way over time.

**The self-evolving agent problem.** If agents can research, evaluate, and improve their own orchestration autonomously, the taste and distribution moats erode. The agent doesn't need a human curator if it can curate for itself. This is speculative but not science fiction — early versions of agent self-improvement loops already exist. The counter-counter: self-evolving agents still need context to evolve against, and someone needs to define what "good" looks like. The evolution engine's quality scoring IS that definition.

**The incumbent complacency parallel.** Moats require active defense. Kodak owned photography distribution. Blockbuster owned home entertainment trust. IBM owned enterprise context. The evolution engine's moats are only as durable as the effort maintaining them. Accumulating artefacts without productizing them is like owning a gold mine without building a refinery.

## Conclusion

The mapping reveals something I didn't expect when I started the evolution project: it's not internal tooling. It's moat infrastructure. The context accumulation (95%), taste codification (85%), and trust provenance (40%) represent genuine structural advantages that AI commoditizes other things toward, not away from. The weakest position is distribution (60%) — the intelligence is trapped inside the system.

The sharpest implication is strategic, not technical. The evolution project currently optimizes for completeness — 8 domains, 9 factories, 5/5 DNA score. The moats framework says it should optimize for concentration — deepen the strongest positions (context and taste) and open them to external consumption before the accumulated intelligence becomes the kind of over-engineered asset that looks impressive but produces no revenue.

## Implications

Three roadmap shifts, ranked by moat impact:

**Deprioritize capability plays.** Chat assistant expansion and Kestra orchestration setup are middleware — building capability that base models will commoditize. Keep them on the roadmap, but move them below moat-building work.

**Prioritize the experience layer and knowledge compound loop.** The experience layer (tracking research outcomes as routing intelligence) and the knowledge-base-evolution compound loop are context moat plays. They deepen the data gravity that no competitor can replicate. Move them to the top of the roadmap.

**Build distribution.** The evolution engine's accumulated intelligence — quality-scored prompts, signal-optimized menus, effectiveness-rated skills — needs to be exposed as a queryable, trust-verified API. This is the agent discovery problem that Jones identifies as one of the most interesting questions of 2026. The system that solves it for its own accumulated intelligence owns the distribution layer for that specific vertical.

The bottom line: the evolution project is accidentally building exactly the kind of structural advantage that survives AI commoditization. The question isn't whether to keep building it. It's whether to keep building it as internal infrastructure or start building it as a product with a moat. The clock is running.
