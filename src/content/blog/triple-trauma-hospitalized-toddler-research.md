# The Triple Trauma: What Happens When Hospitals Separate, Restrain, and Operate on Toddlers

What happens to a two-year-old who is taken from their mother, operated on without a parent present, and physically restrained to stop them touching their stitches? Until the 1950s, this was routine pediatric practice across the Western world. New research shows the scars persist into adulthood — and that restraint remains far more common than anyone assumed.

## The Evidence Base

This synthesis draws from 20 ingested research sources, 58 extracted entities, 20 confirmed facts, and 5 peer-reviewed studies including three new additions:

- **de Souza et al. (2026)** — First large-scale prospective study: **22.3% of pediatric procedures involve physical restraint**. Younger children are significantly more affected.
- **Dalton et al. (2023)** — CHOP clinicians confirm restraint causes both physical and psychological harm; hospital environment itself drives agitation.
- **van der Horst & van der Veer (2009)** — Historical analysis showing the reform movement was broader than just Bowlby and Robertson.

## The Triple Trauma Model

When a child aged 1-3 is hospitalized for surgery, three traumatic stressors converge simultaneously:

| Factor | Primary Effect | Long-term Consequence |
|--------|---------------|----------------------|
| **Maternal Separation** | Protest-Despair-Detachment (Bowlby) | Attachment disruption, relationship difficulties |
| **Surgical Pain** | CNS rewiring, pain hypersensitivity | Chronic pain conditions, somatic symptoms |
| **Physical Restraint** | Learned helplessness, loss of agency | Emotional numbing, trust destruction |

The interaction is **multiplicative, not additive**. Each factor alone causes measurable distress; combined, they overwhelm the developing stress-response system.

## The PDD Response: What Separation Actually Looks Like

Bowlby and Robertson documented three phases young children pass through when separated from their primary caregiver:

**Phase 1: Protest** (hours to days) — Active crying, screaming, thrashing. The child searches for their caregiver, rejects substitutes. Physiological markers include elevated cortisol and tachycardia.

**Phase 2: Despair** (days to weeks) — Withdrawal, reduced movement, intermittent crying. Self-soothing behaviors emerge: thumb-sucking, rocking, repetitive motions. Staff typically misinterpret this quiet as the child having settled — in reality, they are shutting down.

**Phase 3: Detachment** (weeks+) — Apparent contentment. The child treats all adults interchangeably, shows emotional flatness. This is not recovery — it is attachment system shutdown. Detachment beyond 14 days has a high probability of becoming permanent.

Robertson filmed two-year-old Laura over eight days of hospitalization in 1952. The film upset doctors and nurses so much that it eventually contributed to the government forming the Platt Committee.

## Physical Restraint: The Practice Nobody Talked About

In the 1940s-1970s, restraining hospitalized toddlers was routine. Arms were tied down using splints, bandages, straps, or papoose boards to prevent children from interfering with stitches, IVs, or bandages. Parents were excluded from wards — visiting was often restricted to one hour per week.

The CHEOPS pain scale, developed in the 1980s, includes "restrained" as a scoring category — reflecting how normalized this practice was.

### New Evidence: Restraint Is Still Common

De Souza et al. (2026) observed 1,210 procedures on 75 children (ages 28 days to 10 years) in a Brazilian public hospital. Key findings:

- Physical restraint was used in **22.3% of all procedures** (270/1,210)
- 48.5% of painful procedures involved restraint
- 51.5% of stressful procedures involved restraint
- Younger children were significantly more likely to be restrained
- Stress behaviors were observed **before** restraint was initiated

The authors explicitly call for reframing restraint "from an unquestioned practice toward an approach that prioritises protecting and respecting a child as a subject with needs, rights, and desires."

Dalton et al. (2023) interviewed 40 clinicians at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia and found that hospital environment (bright lights, noise, unfamiliarity) directly contributes to agitation, and that team communication culture is the strongest modifiable factor in avoiding restraint.

## Learned Helplessness: Why Restraint Compounds Everything

When a toddler protests (screams, thrashes) and is forcibly restrained, they learn that resistance is futile. This mirrors Seligman's learned helplessness model:

1. **Controllability loss** — crying, thrashing, and protesting do not bring the caregiver back or stop the pain
2. **Response-outcome independence** — nothing the child does matters
3. **Cognitive generalization** — "I cannot affect ANY situation"
4. **Motivational deficit** — the child stops trying to influence their environment
5. **Emotional deficit** — flat affect, reduced distress signals (misinterpreted as "settled")

The 1-3 age range is particularly devastating because agency — the sense that one can act on the world — is developing precisely during this period. Hospitalization with restraint strikes at the most sensitive moment for developing personal efficacy.

## The Historical Reform Movement

The fight to change hospital practice took decades. Van der Horst & van der Veer (2009) show that Bowlby and Robertson were influential but not the only reformers. The change was driven by:

- Worried medical doctors who noticed children's distress
- Pressure groups of parents demanding change (NAWCH)
- Sympathetic medical journal editors
- Individual hospitals that had already introduced humane practices
- Robertson's 1952 film "A Two-Year-Old Goes to Hospital"

Robertson and Dermod MacCarthy showed their films directly to the Platt Committee in 1957-58. The resulting **Platt Report (1959)** recommended unrestricted parental visiting and mother accommodation — revolutionizing pediatric care in the UK.

## The Compounding Effect

Each trauma amplifies the others in a vicious cycle:



The child trapped in this cycle:
1. Is separated from their only source of comfort
2. Experiences uncontrolled pain they cannot understand
3. Is physically prevented from self-soothing or resisting
4. Has no cognitive ability to contextualize what is happening
5. Has no advocate present

## What This Means

This research matters because understanding the mechanism is the first step toward addressing its effects. The adults who experienced this triple trauma as toddlers did not choose their attachment patterns, their pain responses, or their emotional regulation strategies. These were shaped by experiences they could not control, during the most vulnerable period of human development.

The historical record shows that change IS possible — the Platt Report transformed pediatric care. But de Souza's 2026 data shows restraint remains common. The work of reform is ongoing.
