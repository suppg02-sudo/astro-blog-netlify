# The Architect of Software 2.0: The Life and Legacy of Andrej Karpathy

In February 2025, a single tweet from a Slovak-Canadian AI researcher gave the world a new vocabulary. "Vibe coding," Andrej Karpathy wrote, describing how AI tools let anyone build software just by typing natural language prompts. The term exploded overnight. It captured something essential about the new relationship between humans and machines — a relationship Karpathy himself had spent two decades building from the ground up.

This is the story of a kid from Bratislava who became one of the most influential minds in artificial intelligence — a researcher who shaped Tesla's self-driving vision, co-founded OpenAI, coined the defining software paradigm of our era, and then walked away from it all to teach.

## From Bratislava to Toronto

Andrej Karpathy was born on 23 October 1986 in Bratislava, Czechoslovakia — a city that would become the capital of Slovakia just five years later after the Velvet Divorce. When he was 15, his family emigrated to Toronto, Canada. It was a move that would place him at the epicentre of a revolution.

Toronto in the early 2000s was quietly becoming the cradle of modern deep learning. Geoffrey Hinton, the godfather of backpropagation, held court at the University of Toronto, and the young Karpathy walked straight into his orbit. Enrolling in a double major of Computer Science and Physics with a minor in Mathematics, Karpathy attended Hinton's lectures and reading groups — absorbing ideas that most of the world wouldn't encounter for another decade.

But there were hints of the educator he'd become even then. In 2006, Karpathy started a YouTube channel called *badmephisto*, posting Rubik's Cube tutorials. The videos became legendary in the speedcubing community — Feliks Zemdegs, one of the greatest speedcubers of all time, learned from them. The channel eventually amassed over 9 million views. Long before he taught Stanford students or YouTube millions about neural networks, Karpathy was already obsessed with breaking complex things down so others could understand them.

## The Academic Crucible

After completing his BSc at Toronto in 2009, Karpathy moved west to the University of British Columbia for his master's degree, working with Michiel van de Panne on machine learning for physically simulated figures — teaching virtual runners and crowd members how to move. It was an early signal of his fascination with learning systems that could interact with the physical world.

But the real crucible was Stanford. Starting his PhD in 2011 under the supervision of Fei-Fei Li at the Stanford Vision Lab, Karpathy threw himself into the intersection of computer vision and natural language processing — at the time, a relatively niche corner of AI. His dissertation, *Connecting Images and Natural Language* (2016), tackled a problem that would later become central to modern AI: how do you get a machine to understand what it sees and describe it in words?

Along the way, he squeezed in three defining internships: Google Brain in 2011 (unsupervised learning from video), Google Research in 2013 (large-scale supervised learning on YouTube), and DeepMind in 2015 (deep reinforcement learning). Each one placed him at the frontier of what neural networks could do.

Then came CS 231n.

## The Course That Changed Everything

In 2015, Karpathy designed and became the primary instructor of Stanford's first deep learning course: *CS 231n: Convolutional Neural Networks for Visual Recognition*. It started with 150 students. By 2017, it had ballooned to 750 — one of the largest classes at Stanford University.

This wasn't just a popular course. CS 231n became the template for how deep learning would be taught worldwide. Its lecture notes, freely available online, became canonical references. A generation of AI engineers cut their teeth on Karpathy's explanations of convolutional layers, backpropagation, and gradient descent. The course notes at cs231n.github.io remain, to this day, one of the most cited educational resources in the field.

Karpathy wasn't just teaching — he was building the community that would power the AI revolution.

## The OpenAI Founding

In December 2015, a group of researchers and entrepreneurs announced the formation of OpenAI, a research laboratory dedicated to ensuring artificial general intelligence would benefit all of humanity. Karpathy was there from day one — a founding member and research scientist.

This was the Wild West era of modern AI. The transformer architecture hadn't been invented yet. GPT was a glimmer in researchers' eyes. What OpenAI had was ambition, talent, and a conviction that deep learning could go much further than anyone expected.

Karpathy's work during this period spanned computer vision, generative models, and reinforcement learning. He co-authored papers like *PixelCNN++* and *World of Bits*, pushing the boundaries of what neural networks could generate and interact with. But bigger things were coming.

## Tesla: Building Software 2.0

In June 2017, Elon Musk personally recruited Karpathy from OpenAI to become Tesla's Director of Artificial Intelligence and Autopilot Vision. It was, in many ways, the perfect marriage: Karpathy's deep expertise in computer vision applied to one of the most ambitious real-world AI challenges ever attempted — making cars drive themselves.

At Tesla, Karpathy led the computer vision team responsible for Tesla Autopilot and, briefly, the Tesla Optimus humanoid robot. His team handled everything in-house: data labeling, neural network training, and deployment on Tesla's custom inference chip. Under his leadership, Tesla's vision stack evolved from a promising prototype into a system that hundreds of thousands of drivers use daily.

It was during this period that Karpathy articulated what would become one of his most influential ideas: **Software 2.0**.

In a November 2017 essay, he argued that neural networks represented a fundamental shift in how software gets written. In Software 1.0, programmers write explicit instructions. In Software 2.0, programmers define the objectives and constraints, and optimization finds the code. The "code" in Software 2.0 is the weights of a neural network — billions of parameters discovered through training, not written by human hands.

The essay was prophetic. It reframed not just Tesla's Autopilot approach but the entire trajectory of the AI industry. Today, the largest and most impactful software systems in the world — from ChatGPT to Tesla's FSD — are fundamentally Software 2.0 systems.

Karpathy presented Tesla's progress at AI Day 2021, showcasing the massive data pipeline, neural network architecture, and training infrastructure that powered Autopilot. It was a masterclass in applied AI at scale.

After a several-month sabbatical, Karpathy announced his departure from Tesla in July 2022. His work there had fundamentally reshaped how the automotive industry thinks about AI.

## The Return to OpenAI

In February 2023, just months after ChatGPT had stunned the world, Karpathy announced he was returning to OpenAI. This time, the landscape had transformed entirely. Large language models were now the most important technology on Earth, and Karpathy was back in the building where GPT had been born.

He built a new team focused on midtraining and synthetic data generation — the crucial but often invisible work of preparing the data that makes large language models smart. Synthetic data, the idea of using AI to generate training data for AI, is one of the most important developments in the field, and Karpathy was at its forefront.

His return also produced one of his most-watched presentations: *State of GPT* at Microsoft Build 2023, where he gave a lucid, comprehensive walkthrough of how models like GPT-4 are trained, from data collection through reinforcement learning from human feedback. The presentation became a reference point for anyone trying to understand the mechanics behind modern AI.

But this second stint at OpenAI would be short-lived. In February 2024, Karpathy departed once again — this time, it turned out, for something entirely his own.

## Eureka Labs: The Teacher Returns

On 16 July 2024, Karpathy announced Eureka Labs, an AI education company. If his career had traced an arc — from student to researcher to practitioner to leader — Eureka Labs was the closing of the circle. The man who had taught CS 231n, who had inspired millions through YouTube, was now building a company dedicated to reinventing education for the age of AI.

Eureka Labs' vision is bold: AI teaching assistants that can personally tutor every student, augmenting human teachers rather than replacing them. Their first product, LLM101n: *Let's Build A Storyteller*, walks students through building a large language model from scratch.

The concept hasn't been without critics — concerns about data privacy and the loss of human connection in education are real. But Karpathy's track record as an educator is hard to argue with.

In parallel, his YouTube channel has become a phenomenon in its own right. The *Neural Networks: Zero to Hero* series takes viewers from basic concepts to building GPT from scratch in raw Python. His *Deep Dive into LLMs like ChatGPT* video is perhaps the single best general-audience explanation of how modern language models work. His video *How I Use LLMs* shows the practical side — how one of the world's leading AI researchers actually uses these tools in daily life.

## The Signature Style

What makes Karpathy different from other prominent AI researchers isn't just technical brilliance — it's a rare combination of skills.

**Builder first.** His GitHub is a treasure trove of projects built from scratch: *micrograd* (a tiny autograd engine), *char-rnn* (character-level language models), *arxiv-sanity* (a tool for navigating research papers), *neuraltalk2* (image captioning), and *ConvNetJS* (deep learning in JavaScript, years before it was trendy). He doesn't just theorize — he implements.

**Teacher always.** Whether it's CS 231n at Stanford, a YouTube video with millions of views, or a blog post about training neural networks, Karpathy's instinct is always to explain. His writing has a clarity that comes from genuine deep understanding — he doesn't hide behind jargon or mathematical notation when plain English will do.

**Philosophical edge.** The *Software 2.0* essay wasn't just a technical observation — it was a philosophical framework. His concept of "vibe coding" wasn't just a catchy phrase — it captured a genuine shift in how software gets made. Even his tongue-in-cheek bio ("I like to train deep neural nets on large datasets") and his eccentric claim to "Unicorn Magic" reveal someone who doesn't take himself too seriously, even as he changes the world.

**Principled craft.** Karpathy's famous blog post *A Recipe for Training Neural Networks* reads like a manifesto for careful, methodical engineering: "Don't be a hero." Start simple. Verify each step. Build up complexity gradually. It's advice that applies far beyond neural networks — it's a philosophy of engineering.

## The Impact

The numbers tell part of the story. MIT Technology Review named him one of their Innovators Under 35 in 2020. TIME Magazine named him one of the 100 Most Influential People in AI in 2024. His research papers have thousands of citations. His YouTube videos have tens of millions of views.

But the deeper impact is harder to quantify. An entire generation of AI engineers learned deep learning from his course notes. Tesla's approach to autonomous driving, now being studied and imitated by every major automaker, bears his architectural fingerprints. The vocabulary we use to talk about AI — Software 2.0, vibe coding — is partly his vocabulary. And through Eureka Labs, he's positioning himself to shape how the next generation learns AI.

## The Man Behind the Code

There's a wonderful moment in Karpathy's online presence that captures something essential. On his personal website, alongside his CV and publications, there's a page where he's sorted all the science fiction books he's ever read. There's his old Rubik's Cube channel. There's a Tumblr of funny loss functions. There are projects for multiplayer cooperative Tetris and predator-prey simulations.

This is someone who builds things for the joy of building them — and then can't help but share what he's learned.

In his PhD survival guide, written in 2016, he offered this advice: "Keep things simple. Don't be a hero. Grad student descent is a thing." It's the same advice he'd give about training neural networks. The same advice implicit in *micrograd*, which implements backpropagation in roughly 150 lines of Python. The same advice that drives Eureka Labs.

Andrej Karpathy's career spans the full arc of modern AI — from Hinton's lecture halls to Tesla's factory floors, from OpenAI's research labs to YouTube's global classroom. Through it all, the thread is consistent: understand deeply, build carefully, explain clearly. In an era of AI hype and breathless predictions, that's a legacy worth paying attention to.
