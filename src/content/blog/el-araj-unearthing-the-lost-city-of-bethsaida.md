In the quiet Betiha Nature Preserve on the northeastern shore of the Sea of Galilee, a remarkable archaeological discovery is reshaping our understanding of biblical history. Since 2016, researchers at El-Araj have been excavating what many now believe to be the ancient city of Bethsaida-Julias—the hometown of the apostles Peter, Andrew, and Philip, and one of the most significant locations in Jesus' ministry.

## The Quest for Bethsaida

For centuries, the exact location of biblical Bethsaida remained a mystery. The city appears in all four Gospels as the birthplace of three of Jesus' closest disciples and the site of numerous miracles, including the healing of a blind man and the feeding of the 5,000. Yet despite its importance, the ancient city seemingly vanished from the historical record after the first few centuries of the Common Era.

The search for Bethsaida has long centered on two competing sites: **e-Tell** (excavated since 1987) and the more recently explored **El-Araj**. While e-Tell boasts impressive Iron Age remains, it lacks clear evidence of first-century Roman occupation—a crucial requirement for New Testament Bethsaida.

Enter El-Araj.

## The El-Araj Excavation Project

Led by Dr. Mordechai Aviam of Kinneret Academic College and Dr. R. Steven Notley of Nyack College, the El-Araj Excavation Project began systematic exploration in 2016. Sponsored by the Museum of the Bible since 2021, the team has made discoveries that have dramatically strengthened the case for El-Araj as the true site of biblical Bethsaida-Julias.

What they've uncovered tells a compelling story of transformation—from humble Jewish fishing village to thriving Roman polis, to revered Christian pilgrimage site.

## A Village by the Sea: First Century CE

Beneath layers of later occupation, archaeologists found exactly what biblical scholars had been searching for: a modest first-century fishing village.

The evidence is unmistakable:
- **Hundreds of lead fishing weights and hooks** scattered throughout the residential area
- **Domestic buildings with plastered floors**—the homes of fishing families
- **Stone vessels** characteristic of Jewish settlements (indicating observance of Jewish purity laws)
- **Herodian oil lamps** manufactured in Jerusalem, suggesting the villagers made pilgrimages to the Temple
- **First-century coins**, including a silver denarius from Emperor Nero (65-66 CE)

Perhaps most remarkable was the discovery of a **Roman-period inkwell** found in one fisherman's house alongside scores of lead fishing weights. This rare find suggests literacy among the villagers—a fascinating glimpse into the social fabric of the fishing community that produced the first apostles.

## From Fishing Village to Roman City

According to the Jewish historian Josephus (Antiquities 18:28), Herod Philip—the son of Herod the Great—transformed Bethsaida into a small Roman polis and renamed it Julias around 30 CE. The excavations at El-Araj provide spectacular archaeological confirmation of this account.

In 2017, the team unearthed an **advanced Roman-style bathhouse** complete with mosaic flooring featuring a white and black meander pattern. The bathhouse included clay bricks and ceramic vents (*tubuli*)—typical features of Roman bathing complexes. This sophisticated urban infrastructure demonstrates that El-Araj was no simple fishing village, but rather a developing Roman city.

Additional evidence of urbanization includes paved streets and private homes, all dating to the 1st-3rd centuries CE—the precise period when Bethsaida-Julias would have been thriving.

## The Church of the Apostles

Perhaps the most compelling evidence emerged in the Byzantine layers: a large basilica built atop the Roman ruins. What makes this church so significant is not just its impressive architecture, but what was found within it.

### The Peter Inscription

During the fifth excavation season, archaeologists discovered **three Greek mosaic inscriptions** in the church floor. One complete inscription reads:

> "Constantine, servant of Christ, established the diakonikon for the chief of the apostles and the keeper of the keys to heaven."

This language directly references the biblical description of Peter (Matthew 16:19), providing explicit textual evidence that the church was dedicated to the apostle Peter. Another inscription appears to commemorate Apostle Andrew, suggesting the church honored both brothers from Bethsaida.

### Layers of Veneration

Beneath the Byzantine basilica, excavators found even more intriguing evidence of early Christian veneration:
- A **first-century wall** within the church's apse
- A **venerated second-century wall** perpendicular to the first-century structure

These earlier walls suggest that Christians began marking and venerating this site as early as the second century—only decades after the New Testament period.

## The August 2025 Revelation

In a remarkable turn of events, a wildfire swept through the Betiha Nature Reserve in late July 2025, burning away dense vegetation that had covered much of the archaeological site. When archaeologists returned to survey the damage, they made an unexpected discovery.

The fire had exposed **small mounds scattered across hundreds of meters**—each representing an ancient room or building. According to Dr. Mordechai Aviam, these mounds represent residential areas of the biblical village. Among the newly exposed features, excavators found a pillar drum, indicating the presence of a public building.

The fire stopped miraculously short of the early church structure (though equipment containers were melted), providing the first extensive survey opportunity in years and promising further revelations about the village layout.

## Why El-Araj Beats e-Tell

The evidence comparing El-Araj with the competing site of e-Tell strongly favors El-Araj as the true biblical Bethsaida:

### El-Araj's Strengths:
- **Clear first-century occupation** with abundant artifacts (coins, pottery, fishing equipment)
- **Roman bathhouse**—evidence of urbanization as Julias
- **Byzantine church with Peter inscription**—matching 8th-century Bishop Willibald's account of a church dedicated to Peter at Bethsaida
- **Geographical alignment** with the Sea of Galilee—perfect for a fishing village
- **Direct textual evidence** (mosaic inscriptions) confirming the site's Christian veneration

### e-Tell's Limitations:
- **No substantial Roman period layer** (1st century CE) despite decades of excavation
- Located further inland (~3 km north of El-Araj), less suitable for a fishing village
- Lacks evidence of first-century occupation matching the biblical period
- No early Christian church or inscriptions

## The Stratigraphy of History

The excavations at El-Araj reveal a remarkable sequence of occupation spanning over 1,500 years:

1. **Late Hellenistic Period (2nd century BCE)**: Initial settlement established
2. **First Century CE**: Jewish fishing village (time of Jesus and the apostles)
3. **30 CE**: Urbanization as Bethsaida-Julias by Herod Philip
4. **1st-3rd Centuries CE**: Roman polis with bathhouse and public buildings
5. **Around 3rd Century CE**: Site inundated (silt layer discovered, aligning with historical records)
6. **4th Century CE**: Fourth-century monument commemorating the apostles' house
7. **5th-8th Centuries CE**: Byzantine church and monastic complex
8. **Crusader Period**: Sugar factory on the site
9. **Mamluk and Ottoman Periods**: Way station for Muslim pilgrims

This complete sequence matches perfectly with the historical accounts from Josephus, the Gospels, and early Christian pilgrimage itineraries.

## The Significance for Biblical Archaeology

The identification of El-Araj as biblical Bethsaida represents one of the most important discoveries in New Testament archaeology of the 21st century. The site provides:

- **Physical confirmation** of biblical narratives about Jesus' ministry in Galilee
- **Archaeological evidence** of the apostolic hometown, bringing the Gospel stories into sharper historical focus
- **Demonstration** of early Christian veneration practices dating to the second century
- **Validation** of Josephus's accounts about Herod Philip's urbanization policies
- **Crossroads of Jewish, Roman, and Christian history** in one remarkable location

## What's Next

The excavation continues through 2026, with plans to:
- Complete excavation of the Roman-period bathhouse
- Uncover more first-century residential structures
- Further explore the early Christian layers beneath the Byzantine church

Each season promises new revelations about this extraordinary site that connects the ancient world of the Gospels with the tangible archaeological record.

## Conclusion

The discoveries at El-Araj have provided compelling evidence that we have indeed found the lost city of Bethsaida-Julias—the home of Peter, Andrew, and Philip, and a significant location in Jesus' ministry.

What makes El-Araj so convincing is not any single discovery, but the convergence of multiple lines of evidence: first-century fishing artifacts, Roman urban infrastructure, Byzantine church inscriptions, and geographical alignment—all matching the historical, biblical, and archaeological requirements for the ancient city.

As excavations continue, El-Araj promises to yield even more insights into the world of the New Testament, bringing us closer to understanding the historical context of early Christianity and the communities that shaped it.

The lost city has been found. And with each season of excavation, its story continues to unfold.
