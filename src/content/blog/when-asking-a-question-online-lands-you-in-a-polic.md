# When Asking a Question Online Lands You in a Police Station: The Alarming Case of Andrew Bridgen

Imagine sharing a video on social media—a video you didn't create, mind you—and simply asking your followers whether the person in it "has a point." Seems harmless enough, right? Now imagine that this simple act results in you sitting in a police station, under caution, being interviewed by officers investigating you for a potential crime.

This isn't a dystopian fiction. This is exactly what happened to former MP Andrew Bridgen, and according to legal experts, it's something that could happen to any one of us.

## The Case That Should Concern Everyone

In January 2026, Andrew Bridgen shared an AI-generated video on Twitter featuring a fictional character named "Amelia." His commentary was remarkably neutral: he simply asked whether she "had a point" and encouraged others to watch and share if they thought so.

The video itself wasn't even original content. It was publicly available and featured a character called Amelia—a character, by the way, that was originally created by the Home Office as part of a game designed to identify children who might be vulnerable to radicalization. The video Bridgen shared was a satirical, anti-government piece that repurposed this official character.

For this—for sharing a publicly available video and asking a question—Bridgen found himself sitting in a police station.

## Voluntary Interviews: A Misleading Name

Here's something many people don't understand: a "voluntary" police interview carries exactly the same legal weight as an interview following an arrest. You're still interviewed under caution. Everything you say can be used against you in a prosecution. The consequences are identical; the only difference is the optics.

Bridgen understood this all too well. In his prepared statement, he explicitly noted his distrust of the police to interview him fairly, citing previous experiences. This wasn't paranoia—it was strategic legal thinking.

## The Allegation: "Grossly Offensive"

According to the prosecution's outline (the MG5 document), the allegation was that the AI-generated female character in the video "makes racist or homophobic comments and would be deemed grossly offensive to a reasonable person."

Let that sink in. The threshold for police intervention in this country has apparently dropped to whether something *might be deemed* offensive. Not whether it incites violence. Not whether it causes actual harm. But whether a hypothetical "reasonable person" might find it grossly offensive.

The case was eventually dropped—police took no further action. But the chilling effect had already been achieved. A former Member of Parliament was hauled in for questioning over a social media post. If it can happen to him, it can happen to you.

## The Right to Remain Silent—With Consequences

Bridgen's approach to the interview offers a masterclass in legal strategy. He provided a prepared statement and then answered "no comment" to every subsequent question.

This approach isn't without risk. In both magistrates' court and crown court proceedings, negative inferences *can* be drawn from a defendant's refusal to answer questions. However, as any competent barrister will tell you, these inferences can be effectively countered by explaining *why* you remained silent.

In Bridgen's case, his prepared statement made clear that his silence wasn't an attempt to hide wrongdoing—it was a response to his legitimate concerns about unfair treatment. This transforms a potential weakness into a defensible position.

## The Bigger Picture: Free Speech Under Siege

This case exemplifies a troubling trend in modern policing. We're seeing increasing intervention in online speech, often under the guise of preventing offense. Meanwhile, as one legal commentator noted, the fundamental infrastructure of the country seems to be crumbling around us—trains delayed or cancelled, potholes everywhere, people waiting months for essential medical procedures.

Yet somehow, sharing an AI video and asking whether it "has a point" warrants police time and resources.

The irony is particularly rich given that the character in question was created by the government itself. The Home Office designed Amelia. They put her into the world. And now someone is being investigated for sharing a video that uses her image in a way the government didn't intend.

## What You Need to Know

If you're active on social media, you need to understand the legal landscape:

**Voluntary interviews are serious business.** Never treat them casually. Seek legal advice immediately if you're contacted for one.

**Prepared statements matter.** If you have legitimate concerns about fair treatment, document them clearly before any interview.

**The "grossly offensive" standard is worryingly subjective.** What seems reasonable to you might be interpreted very differently by someone determined to find offense.

**Your silence has consequences—but so does speaking.** Every word you say in a police interview can be dissected and used against you. Strategic silence, properly explained, is often the wisest course.

## The Bottom Line

We're living in a moment where the simple act of asking a question online—"Do you think she has a point?"—can result in a police interview. Regardless of your political views, regardless of whether you agree with what was shared, this should concern you deeply.

The right to freedom of expression exists precisely to protect speech that some might find uncomfortable or objectionable. Popular speech doesn't need protection. It's the speech at the edges—the controversial, the satirical, the challenging—that defines whether we truly have free expression or merely the illusion of it.

Andrew Bridgen's case was dropped. But the next person might not be so lucky. And that person could be you.

Stay informed. Know your rights. And think carefully before you post, share, or even ask a question online. The consequences might be more serious than you ever imagined.
