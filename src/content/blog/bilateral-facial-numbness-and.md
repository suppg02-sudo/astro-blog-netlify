This document summarises clinical evidence linking progressive bilateral facial numbness, paresthesia, and burning sensations with autoimmune connective tissue disease. It is intended to support a GP consultation by outlining a specific symptom profile, the relevant differential diagnoses, and the published literature that connects these findings.

## Quick Summary

- Bilateral facial numbness affecting the nose, eyebrows, temples, scalp, lips, and ear — with a slow 10+ year progression that has accelerated over the last 6 months
- Additional features: paresthesia, burning sensation, rashes, tinnitus, and Raynaud's phenomenon
- The top differential is **Mixed Connective Tissue Disease (MCTD)** — trigeminal neuropathy is literally a minor diagnostic criterion (#7 in the Sharp classification). Also consider: Limited Cutaneous Systemic Sclerosis, SLE, and Sjogren's syndrome
- Trigeminal neuropathy — including bilateral presentation — is a documented feature of autoimmune connective tissue disease, not exclusively a vascular compression syndrome

**Key clinical features of the progression:**

- Bilateral involvement (both sides of the face)
- Distribution follows trigeminal nerve dermatomes (V1: scalp/forehead/eyebrows, V2: nose/cheeks/upper lip, V3: lower lip/chin)
- No response to standard neuralgia medications in early trials
- Acceleration in the last 6 months suggesting disease evolution

## Why This Is Likely Not Classic Trigeminal Neuralgia

Classic trigeminal neuralgia (TN) — also known as tic douloureux — presents as unilateral, brief, electric-shock-like pain triggered by light touch. It is typically caused by vascular compression of the trigeminal nerve root.

This symptom profile differs from classic TN in several important ways:

1. **Bilateral involvement**: Classic TN is almost always unilateral. Bilateral presentation is rare, and when it occurs, it typically indicates a systemic cause. The Wikipedia entry on trigeminal neuralgia notes: "Bilateral trigeminal neuralgia is very rare except for trigeminal neuralgia caused by multiple sclerosis (MS). This normally indicates problems with both trigeminal nerves." While MS is one systemic cause, autoimmune connective tissue diseases are another.

2. **Numbness rather than lancinating pain**: The predominant symptom is progressive numbness and paresthesia, not the paroxysmal shock-like pain of classic TN. This is more consistent with **trigeminal neuropathy** (nerve damage/degeneration) rather than neuralgia (nerve irritation).

3. **Slow progressive course**: Classic TN typically has a more abrupt onset with discrete attacks. A 10-year progressive march of numbness suggests a neurodegenerative or inflammatory process.

4. **Associated systemic features**: The presence of Raynaud's phenomenon, rashes, and burning sensations points toward a systemic process rather than an isolated nerve compression.

## The Autoimmune Connection: What the Literature Shows

<details>
<summary><strong>1. Maikap & Padhan (2022) — The Key Paper</strong></summary>

The most directly relevant paper is Maikap and Padhan's 2022 case series published in the *Mediterranean Journal of Rheumatology*: "Trigeminal neuralgia as an initial presentation of systemic autoimmune diseases."

This paper documents three cases where trigeminal neuralgia was the **first presenting symptom** of an underlying autoimmune condition:

- Two patients had Mixed Connective Tissue Disease (MCTD)
- One patient had diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis
- In all cases, the facial nerve symptoms preceded the formal autoimmune diagnosis

The authors reviewed the existing literature on trigeminal neuralgia in connective tissue disease and concluded that TN can be an early herald of systemic autoimmune disease — sometimes appearing years before other diagnostic criteria are met.

This is particularly relevant because the symptom progression described here (10+ years of slow facial neuropathy before systemic features became prominent) mirrors the pattern described in these cases.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>2. Viswanathan et al. (2025) — MCTD Systematic Review</strong></summary>

A 2025 systematic review by Viswanathan and colleagues in the *Indian Journal of Rheumatology* specifically examined neurological manifestations in MCTD. They found that **trigeminal neuralgia was the most common neurological manifestation** of MCTD, reported in 11 studies.

This is significant because MCTD is characterised by overlapping features of multiple autoimmune diseases — including scleroderma, SLE, and polymyositis — with the presence of anti-U1 RNP antibodies.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>3. Poshattiwar et al. (2023) — Neurological Manifestations of CTD</strong></summary>

A comprehensive review in *Cureus* (2023) documented the neurological manifestations across connective tissue disorders. Key findings relevant here:

- **Scleroderma**: Facial nerve palsy, trigeminal neuralgia, and seizures are linked to scleroderma
- **SLE**: Can cause cranial neuropathies including trigeminal nerve involvement
- **Sjogren's syndrome**: Trigeminal neuropathy is one of the most common neurological complications

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>4. Yildirim et al. (2023) — Paediatric-Onset MCTD</strong></summary>

Even in paediatric literature, Yildirim and colleagues documented a case of trigeminal neuropathy as the initial manifestation of MCTD. They noted that the neuropathy did not regress with prednisolone alone, suggesting that the nerve damage may become established if not treated early with disease-modifying agents.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>5. Ferrara et al. (2023) — MCTD as Part of the Scleroderma Spectrum</strong></summary>

Ferrara and colleagues argued that MCTD should be considered part of the scleroderma disease spectrum. This is relevant because the combination of Raynaud's phenomenon (a hallmark of scleroderma spectrum disorders) with facial neuropathy strengthens the case for an underlying scleroderma-spectrum process.

</details>

## Top Differential Diagnoses

Based on the symptom profile and literature, the following conditions should be considered, in order of likelihood:

### 1. Mixed Connective Tissue Disease (MCTD)

**Why it fits:**
- Trigeminal neuropathy is the most common neurological manifestation of MCTD (Viswanathan 2025)
- MCTD overlaps with scleroderma (explaining Raynaud's) and SLE
- Anti-U1 RNP antibodies are the serological marker
- Can present with facial symptoms years before full disease expression
- **Trigeminal neuropathy is a formal diagnostic criterion** — it is listed as minor criterion #7 in the Sharp classification criteria for MCTD
- Sensorineural hearing loss occurs in ~50% of MCTD patients (tinnitus is a common precursor)

**Diagnostic tests**: ENA panel (includes anti-U1 RNP), ANA, complement levels, ESR/CRP

### 2. Limited Cutaneous Systemic Sclerosis (lcSSc)

**Why it fits:**
- Raynaud's phenomenon is nearly universal in lcSSc
- Facial skin thickening and telangiectasia can occur
- Trigeminal neuropathy is documented in scleroderma (Poshattiwar 2023)
- Anti-centromere antibodies are the typical serological marker
- Limited form tends to have a slower progression — consistent with 10+ year history

**Diagnostic tests**: Anti-centromere antibodies, anti-Scl-70, nailfold capillaroscopy, pulmonary function tests

### 3. Sjogren's Syndrome (Primary or Secondary)

**Why it fits:**
- Trigeminal neuropathy is a well-documented complication
- Can cause bilateral facial numbness
- Often overlaps with other CTDs
- Trigeminal neuropathy can occur without sicca symptoms

**Diagnostic tests**: Anti-SSA/Ro, anti-SSB/La antibodies, salivary gland biopsy, Schirmer's test

### 4. Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

**Why it fits:**
- Cranial neuropathies including trigeminal involvement are documented
- Can cause rashes and Raynaud's
- Often overlaps with other CTDs

**Diagnostic tests**: Anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, ANA, complement C3/C4, urinalysis

## Why Bilateral Presentation Supports an Autoimmune Aetiology

The bilateral nature of the symptoms is a critical diagnostic clue. In classic vascular compression TN, symptoms affect only one side because only one nerve root is being compressed. Bilateral involvement strongly suggests a systemic process affecting both trigeminal nerves simultaneously.

The medical literature supports this reasoning:

- Maikap & Padhan (2022) specifically documented bilateral or multi-branch trigeminal involvement in their autoimmune cases
- The *Cureus* review (2023) noted that autoimmune-related trigeminal neuropathy often affects multiple branches (V1, V2, V3) simultaneously — matching the distribution from scalp to lips described here
- Bilateral presentation in the context of Raynaud's phenomenon and rashes creates a clinical picture consistent with systemic autoimmune disease rather than isolated nerve pathology

## Recommended Investigations

**Priority test: ENA panel** — this single blood test covers anti-U1 RNP (MCTD), anti-centromere (lcSSc), anti-SSA/SSB (Sjogren's), anti-Scl-70 (SSc), and anti-Sm/dsDNA (SLE). Request this alongside ANA as first-line.

To pursue or discuss with a GP or rheumatologist:

| Investigation | Rationale | Priority |
|---------------|-----------|----------|
| **ENA panel** | Single test covering all major autoimmune markers (anti-U1 RNP, anti-centromere, anti-SSA/SSB, anti-Scl-70, anti-Sm) | **CRITICAL** |
| ANA (antinuclear antibodies) | First-line screening for autoimmune disease | **HIGH** |
| Anti-dsDNA | Marker for SLE (often included in ENA panel) | HIGH |
| ESR, CRP | Inflammatory markers | HIGH |
| Complement C3, C4 | Low in active autoimmune disease | HIGH |
| MRI brain with contrast | Rule out MS, tumour, or structural causes | HIGH |
| Nailfold capillaroscopy | Detects microvascular changes typical of scleroderma spectrum | MEDIUM |
| Nerve conduction studies | Characterise the trigeminal neuropathy | MEDIUM |
| Rheumatology referral | For comprehensive evaluation and management | HIGH |
