## From Wall Street Insider to Whistleblower

Simon Dixon's obsession with money began when his father lost his entire retirement savings in the dotcom boom and bust 25 years ago. Determined to understand where the money went, Dixon pursued a master's degree in economics — but found academia provided no real answers. He then embedded himself inside the financial machine, starting as a tea boy and rising through the ranks to become a stock broker, then a market maker in the City of London, and eventually working in corporate finance at an investment bank.

What he discovered was disturbing. As a market maker, his job was literally to manipulate share prices. When institutional traders wanted to offload positions, his role was to find retail investors to dump them onto. In corporate finance, he witnessed how companies are financialized and securitized — turned into instruments of subordination where banks control board seats and dictate policy across their portfolio.

## The Financial Industrial Complex Explained

Dixon describes a system he calls the "financial industrial complex" — a network of institutions that work together to subordinate companies, individuals, and governments through debt.

The mechanism works in layers:

1. **Retail banks** create fiat currency every time they issue a loan — the vast majority of money in circulation is created this way, not by central banks or governments
2. **Interest on debt** forces perpetual growth — borrowers must either invest productively or find someone else to take on more debt, creating a self-feeding cycle
3. **Central banks** guarantee the system — when the Ponzi scheme wobbles, taxpayers bail it out through government debt
4. **Corporations** are financialized into equity vehicles that can be borrowed against, creating more debt and more subordination
5. **Governments** become the ultimate debt dump — maintaining a facade of democratic control while answering to lobbying interests

The result is a pay-to-play system where political leaders are essentially empty suits managing different "fast food restaurants" — they may appear to be in charge, but the real power lies with those who control the money supply.

<summary>Deep Dive: The Banking Ponzi Mechanism</summary>

Dixon explains that when a retail bank issues a loan, it doesn't lend out existing deposits — it creates new money. This is the "Wizard of Oz" trick that most people don't understand. The bank creates the principal but not the interest, which means the system as a whole must constantly grow just to service existing debt.

This is why deflation terrifies central bankers — in a deflationary environment, the debt burden grows in real terms while the economy shrinks, exposing the Ponzi nature of the system. The 2008 financial crisis was a near-death experience for this model, and the response — quantitative easing — was simply a larger-scale version of the same trick: creating more debt to service existing debt.

The endgame, according to Dixon, is to load all systemic risk onto government balance sheets while maintaining the illusion of democratic governance. Sovereign debt becomes the ultimate backstop, and taxpayers — present and future — bear the cost through inflation, taxation, and reduced public services.
