Julia McCoy's recent analysis identifies three emerging skills that barely exist today but will command premium compensation within two years. The argument rests on a single economic premise: when one person can do the work of twenty, that person's value isn't inflated — it's arithmetic.

## Quick Summary

- **Agent Orchestration** — Coordinating multiple AI agents into autonomous workflows (not using ChatGPT, but being the "CEO of an AI company")
- **AI-Human Interface Design** — Designing where human judgment enters, how AI communicates uncertainty, and preventing automation bias
- **AI Safety & Alignment Translation** — Bridging the gap between safety research papers and actual product decisions

## Agent Orchestration: From Tool User to System Designer

The shift is from using AI as a calculator to orchestrating it as a company. An orchestrator coordinates research agents, writing agents, coding agents, and planning agents — designing workflows, handoffs, quality checks, and fallback procedures.

What makes this skill scarce isn't technical depth. It requires systems thinking, clear communication, and understanding AI's capabilities and limits. No computer science degree needed. Almost nobody is learning it.

> [!NOTE] Why $500K?
> A skilled orchestrator can replace 10-20 traditional employees by designing autonomous systems that run 24/7 without direct oversight. That's not hype — it's leverage multiplication.

## AI-Human Interface Design: The Hard Problem Nobody's Solving

As AI capability grows, the interaction problem compounds. This isn't UI design — it's the entire human-AI collaboration system:

- Where does human judgment get inserted?
- How does AI communicate uncertainty?
- When does the system escalate to a human?
- How do you prevent automation bias (humans accepting whatever AI says)?

The same problem exists across domains: autonomous vehicles handing control back, AI diagnoses supporting (not replacing) doctors, investment recommendations with transparent risk assumptions, creative assistance without losing human authorship.

> [!WARNING] The Stakes
> Companies that get this wrong face disasters — either AI-caused harm from overtrust, or zero-value AI because humans ignore it entirely.

This requires psychology, design, systems engineering, and domain expertise. Aviation has studied human-automation interaction for decades — that knowledge transfers directly.

## AI Safety & Alignment Translation: The Bridge Role

The gap between AI safety research and practical implementation is massive. Researchers write papers about alignment, interpretability, and robustness. Companies need people who can translate those papers into product decisions, testing procedures, and executive communication.

Three forces driving value:

| Force | Driver | Impact |
|-------|--------|--------|
| **Regulation** | EU AI Act passed, US inevitable | Compliance requires dual fluency |
| **Liability** | AI harm → lawsuits | Documented safety cases are legal armour |
| **Trust** | Competitive differentiator | Genuine safety commitment wins customers |

This is translation work — taking complex technical and philosophical concepts and making them actionable for product teams, executives, and regulators.

## The Learning Path

For all three skills, the approach is consistent:

1. **Deeply understand current AI tools** — not just use them, understand their capabilities and limits
2. **Build progressively complex systems** — connect tools via Zapier, Make, or frameworks
3. **Document what works and what fails** — build a track record
4. **Join boundary-pushing communities** — learn from practitioners, not theorists
5. **Study failure cases** — aviation HCI, AI incident databases, system breakdown analyses
6. **Start now** — no established credentials exist yet; first-movers build the expertise everyone else will chase

## What This Means

The AI economy isn't approaching — it's operational. The skills that matter aren't incremental improvements on existing roles. They're fundamentally new: orchestrating autonomous systems, designing human-machine collaboration, and translating safety research into business reality.

$500K for a single contributor sounds extreme until you calculate the leverage: one person designing systems that replace twenty, preventing liabilities that dwarf salaries, and building trust that becomes competitive advantage.

The window is open because these fields have no gatekeepers, no credentials, no established paths. The people who will command that compensation in 2027 are building their expertise right now.
