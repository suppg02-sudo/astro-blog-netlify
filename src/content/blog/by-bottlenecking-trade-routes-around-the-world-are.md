# By Bottlenecking Trade Routes around the World, Are Energy-Related Lockdowns the End Goal?

If you've been feeling a sense of déjà vu lately, you're not alone. The geopolitical chessboard is shifting once again, and the patterns emerging look suspiciously familiar to anyone who was paying attention during the events of 2020. In a recent discussion on The Solari Report, Catherine Austin Fitts and her guests broke down a troubling possibility: what if the next global "reset" isn't coming in the form of a pandemic, but through strategically orchestrated trade route disruptions and energy crises?

Let's dive into this provocative analysis and explore whether we're witnessing the early stages of what some are calling "COVID 2.0"—an economic restructuring disguised as geopolitical necessity.

## The "Two Weeks" Deception All Over Again

Remember the phrase "two weeks to flatten the curve"? It became a bitter punchline for millions who watched temporary measures stretch into years of disrupted life. According to the Solari Report discussion, we're seeing similar rhetoric emerge from political leadership regarding current conflicts in the Middle East.

The analysis suggests that despite promises of quick resolutions—whether "four weeks" or other optimistic timelines—the reality is that certain geopolitical situations are "existential" in nature and will persist for years. The comparison to pandemic messaging is intentional and pointed. Just as COVID-era promises of brevity proved false, these new assurances may serve the same purpose: keeping the public compliant while longer-term agendas are implemented.

The warning is clear: when you hear "short-term pain, long-term gain" or similar refrains, it's worth maintaining a healthy skepticism about both the timeline and the ultimate objectives.

## Strategic Chokepoints: The New Battleground

Here's where the analysis becomes particularly concerning. The conversation highlights that we're not just looking at isolated regional conflicts, but potentially coordinated disruptions to the world's most critical shipping lanes.

Yemen's involvement in current hostilities raises the specter of Red Sea closures—a route that handles a significant portion of global trade. But the analysis goes further, suggesting that disruptions could expand to other critical chokepoints, including the Panama Canal. The result? A systematic "bottlenecking" of trade routes around the world.

When you control the flow of goods, you control economies. And when energy supplies are threatened—through the Strait of Hormuz, the Suez Canal, or other vital passages—the impact on fuel prices becomes immediate and severe. For everyday citizens, this translates to higher costs for everything from gasoline to groceries, from manufactured goods to heating oil.

## COVID 2.0: Not a Pandemic, But a Playbook

Perhaps the most provocative claim in the discussion is the reframing of what COVID-19 actually represented. The argument isn't about viruses or public health measures—it's about economic engineering.

According to this analysis, COVID-19 was never fundamentally about healthcare policy. Instead, it served as "the ultimate political and economic cover for unprecedented intervention." The pandemic provided the justification for:

- Massive money creation on a scale never before seen
- Direct payments that made populations dependent
- Bailouts that favored certain sectors over others
- The transfer of approximately $5 trillion into select accounts

The contention is stark: the pandemic was a "plunder opportunity." By shutting down small and medium-sized businesses while allowing large corporations to operate under special rules, the crisis facilitated one of the largest wealth transfers in history. The "cover story"—whether health-related or otherwise—matters less than the economic outcomes it produces.

## The Wealth Transfer Mechanism

The Solari Report analysis pulls no punches in describing who benefited and who suffered during the COVID period. The formula was straightforward:

**Winners:** Billionaires, large-scale businesses, and financial institutions that received preferential treatment and access to cheap capital.

**Losers:** Small businesses, medium-sized enterprises, independent workers, and anyone without the political connections or capital to weather indefinite closures.

This wasn't accidental, according to the analysis. It was a feature, not a bug. The crisis provided cover for "buying cheap"—acquiring assets, real estate, and market share at distressed prices while competitors were forced to close their doors permanently.

Understanding this mechanism is crucial because it provides a template for recognizing similar patterns in future "crises."

## The Oil Shock as Economic Cover

The discussion references analysis by Fabio Vighi, who describes what he calls "the programmable crisis." The key insight is this: in a highly indebted economy, a sustained oil shock behaves less like traditional inflation and more like a tax on consumption.

When energy prices spike, every sector of the economy feels the impact. Transportation costs rise, manufacturing becomes more expensive, and consumers have less disposable income after filling their tanks and heating their homes. It's a drag on economic activity that affects everyone—but it affects some more than others.

The chilling suggestion is that an "Iran-induced downturn" could serve "the exact same monetary function as the pandemic." In other words, an energy crisis triggered by Middle East conflict could provide the same cover for monetary intervention, wealth transfers, and economic restructuring that the pandemic provided—just with a different justification.

## Why This Matters Now

If this analysis holds water, the implications are significant. We may be watching the early stages of another coordinated economic transformation, this time using geopolitical conflict and energy disruptions as the pretext rather than public health concerns.

The patterns to watch include:
- Escalating rhetoric around Middle East conflicts with no clear resolution
- Rising fuel prices attributed to "supply chain disruptions"
- New calls for government intervention and support programs
- Further consolidation of businesses and assets by large players
- Increasing control over movement and economic activity

None of this is inevitable, of course. But recognizing patterns is the first step to preparing for various scenarios.

## Conclusion: Questioning the Narrative

The value of analysis like that presented in The Solari Report isn't necessarily in accepting every conclusion at face value. Rather, it's in learning to question the narratives we're given and to look beneath the surface of events to understand who benefits.

Whether you fully embrace this interpretation or not, the questions it raises deserve consideration. Why do crisis narratives so consistently result in the same outcomes—wealth concentration, small business destruction, and expanded government control? Why do temporary measures so often become permanent? And what are the true objectives behind the geopolitical maneuvering we're witnessing?

The coming months and years will provide more data points. The question is whether we'll be paying attention to the patterns—or simply accepting the explanations we're given. As the discussion suggests, when someone tells you it will only take "a few weeks," it might be wise to plan for a much longer timeline.
