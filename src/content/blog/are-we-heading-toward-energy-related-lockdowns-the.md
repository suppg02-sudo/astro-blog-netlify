# Are We Heading Toward Energy-Related Lockdowns? The Hidden Agenda Behind Global Trade Bottlenecks

If you've been feeling a sense of déjà vu lately, you're not alone. According to Catherine Austin Fitts of The Solari Report, the geopolitical maneuvers unfolding today bear a striking resemblance to events we've seen before—and the implications for global trade, energy prices, and economic freedom could be profound.

In a recent discussion, Fitts and her guests explored a provocative theory: that escalating conflicts in the Middle East and disruptions to critical trade routes may not be mere geopolitical accidents, but rather calculated steps toward what some are calling "CO 2.0"—a new phase of economic restructuring disguised as crisis response.

## The "Two Weeks" Promise We've Heard Before

Remember "two weeks to flatten the curve"? That familiar promise from 2020 has seemingly found a new incarnation in foreign policy rhetoric. When political leaders suggest military operations will be brief—perhaps just "four weeks"—Fitts urges listeners to be skeptical.

"This is an existential war for Iran," the discussion notes. "It's going to take as long as it takes—and it's going to take years."

The parallel is striking. Just as temporary pandemic measures stretched into years of disruption, these regional conflicts may be setting the stage for prolonged instability. The message remains consistent: bear with us, endure short-term pain for long-term gain. But according to Fitts, this narrative serves a specific purpose—one that has little to do with the stated objectives.

## The Real Target: Global Trade Routes

Here's where things get particularly interesting. The conversation shifts to Yemen and their potential involvement in widening regional conflict. The implications? If the Red Sea becomes impassable, global fuel prices would inevitably surge.

But Fitts suggests this may only be the beginning. Some analysts, including Michael Jan, predict that disruptions could extend to other critical chokepoints—potentially even the Panama Canal. The result would be a systematic "bottlenecking" of trade routes around the world.

This isn't simply about regional conflicts spiraling out of control. Fitts frames it as potentially deliberate: a coordinated effort to restrict the flow of goods and energy across the globe.

## Understanding "CO 2.0": The Economic Reset Continues

Perhaps the most provocative aspect of this analysis is the reframing of what COVID-19 actually represented. Fitts argues that the pandemic was never really about public health policy.

"CO 2.0 wasn't a pandemic," she explains. "It was a lockdown. It was a global reset. It was an economic event."

From this perspective, the pandemic served as a cover story—an elaborate pretext for unprecedented economic intervention. The discussion points to the creation of approximately $5 trillion, which flowed into bank accounts and ultimately enriched the billionaire class while devastating small and medium-sized businesses.

The pattern, according to this analysis, is clear:

- **Prop up billionaires and large corporations** with special rules and direct support
- **Crush small and medium businesses** through forced closures and regulations
- **Create a plunder opportunity** where assets can be acquired cheaply by those with capital
- **Use a compelling narrative** as cover—whether health-related or security-focused

If this interpretation holds water, then what we're witnessing with current trade disruptions may be "the COVID playbook revisited."

## The Programmable Crisis: Oil Shocks as Economic Tools

The discussion references work by Fabio Vighi, who has written extensively about what he terms "the programmable crisis." His analysis offers a crucial insight: in a highly indebted economy, a sustained oil shock behaves less like traditional inflation and more like a tax on consumption.

This distinction matters enormously. When oil prices spike due to supply disruptions—whether from Middle East conflicts or trade route bottlenecks—the effect ripples through every sector of the economy. Transportation costs rise. Manufacturing becomes more expensive. Food prices increase. The consumer bears the burden, effectively paying a tax that flows upward to energy companies and financial institutions.

Vighi's point about the pandemic providing "the ultimate political and economic cover for unprecedented intervention" is particularly relevant here. Massive money creation, direct payments, and bailouts all occurred under the guise of public health emergency. An "Iran-induced downturn," as the discussion suggests, could serve the exact same monetary function.

## What Comes Next?

If Fitts and her guests are correct, we may be entering a phase where energy-related restrictions become the new lockdown mechanism. Rather than ordering businesses to close, the playbook might involve making energy so expensive that economic activity contracts naturally.

The bottlenecks forming at critical trade routes—the Red Sea, potentially the Panama Canal, and other chokepoints—would serve as the physical mechanism for this squeeze. Meanwhile, the geopolitical narrative provides cover: we must support our allies, respond to threats, ensure security.

Whether this interpretation proves accurate remains to be seen. But the patterns are certainly worth watching. When similar rhetoric appears—"short-term pain, long-term gain," temporary measures that become permanent, crises that conveniently enable wealth transfer upward—perhaps it's wise to look beyond the stated narrative.

## The Bottom Line

The Solari Report's analysis challenges us to see current events through a different lens. Rather than viewing trade disruptions, energy price spikes, and geopolitical conflicts as separate phenomena, we might consider how they interconnect—and whose interests they ultimately serve.

If energy-related lockdowns are indeed the end goal, recognizing the pattern is the first step toward preparing for what may come. The playbook, after all, has been tested before. This time, we have the advantage of having seen it in action.

The question now is whether enough people are paying attention to change the outcome.
