## The Core Question

Can a single political figure's refusal to follow established communication patterns fundamentally disrupt a multi-trillion-dollar financial extraction system? That is the provocative thesis advanced by EM Burlingame, a former algorithmic trading professional and US Special Forces operative, in a wide-ranging interview with independent journalist Brian Urwin. Burlingame argues that the global financial infrastructure depends not merely on market volatility, but on **predictable** volatility — and that Trump's chaotic, unscripted messaging style systematically undermines the information flows upon which algorithmic trading platforms depend.

The claim is sweeping: that the entire architecture of modern social media, data brokerage, and news cycles exists primarily to serve computational trading systems, and that deliberate emotional manipulation of populations generates the predictable information flows from which vast profits are extracted. If true, the implications extend far beyond one presidency.

## The Evidence

### The Algorithmic Trading Backbone

Burlingame's starting point is factual and well-documented: more than 90% of securities transactions on global markets are executed by computers. Algorithmic trading, also known as computational or high-frequency trading, dominates market activity. He claims firsthand experience in this space from the early 2000s, describing how the competitive advantage shifted from computational efficiency to information proximity:

> "We weren't going to get a computational communications or a software efficiency advantage anymore over our competitive algorithmic trading platforms because they were investing just as much money in hiring the same brilliant people straight out of university."

By the late 2000s, Burlingame alleges, the logical endpoint became clear: rather than reacting to the news cycle, participants would need to **create** it. "We were already talking about we're going to have to actually create the news to have an advantage in the financial trading system," he states, referencing his time back in Special Forces in late 2008.

### Data Brokers as the Control Layer

The mechanism, according to Burlingame, operates through data brokerage platforms — the same infrastructure commonly associated with digital advertising. He references Antonio Garcia Martinez's *Chaos Monkeys* to illustrate how platforms like Twitter and Facebook built data brokerage systems that match advertisers to digital properties billions of times per second. But the real purpose, he contends, is not advertising revenue:

> "The reason social media was invested in so heavily and blown up so much is so that we could get an advantage in algorithmic trading by being able to read some decision maker in some entity, some organization's thinking — even at a family post."

The architecture works as follows: ad spending is used to amplify certain narratives while suppressing others. Decision-makers consume this curated information environment, make predictable choices, and those choices move markets in ways that pre-positioned algorithmic trades capture — sometimes in milliseconds, sometimes after trades that take months or years to mature.

### The Volatility Requirement

Central to Burlingame's thesis is the distinction between chaos and **managed** chaos. The system requires volatility for pricing, risk premiums, and arbitrage. But the volatility must be predictable enough for algorithms to exploit:

> "They need the predictable volatility. And they need volatility everywhere, at every level, every scale, 24 hours a day, because their machines are trading on that. And what is that ultimately? It's like the alchemist's dream — only you're not turning lead into gold. You're turning computation and information flows that you're manipulating and controlling into returns."

This is where Trump enters the analysis. His unpredictable Truth Social posts, sudden policy announcements, and general refusal to follow diplomatic norms inject genuinely unpredictable information into markets that depend on predictability. According to Burlingame, people around Trump — specifically mentioning those who "know how this works" including "the DOGE people" — are deliberately deploying what he calls the "BBC approach": brutal, blunt, and chaotic.

### The Geopolitical Dimension

Burlingame extends the analysis into geopolitics with several striking claims. He describes Hezbollah as "the investment bank for the devil's legions," alleging it functions as a financial clearing house for criminal networks globally, with significant presence in Paraguay, Nigeria, and Central America. He credits its creation to France, placing it within the historical framework of British and French imperial legacy assets.

Most controversially, he argues that the controlling faction in Israel and the IRGC in Iran are "the same player" — not separate antagonists but complementary chaos agents:

> "They are the same chaos agent in that part of the world. Both created by France and England."

The practical effect, he argues, is that conflicts between them generate the volatility premium from which financial actors profit. The Strait of Hormuz, for example, functions as a "choke point" where chaos premiums are added to every oil shipment. Removing those chaos agents — through targeted strikes rather than prolonged occupation — reduces manipulated pricing and enables genuine price discovery.

### The Intelligence Manipulation Layer

Perhaps the most troubling claim involves the manipulation of intelligence databases. Burlingame alleges that special operations forces across the Five Eyes alliance operate from shared databases controlled by the intelligence community, and that these databases are "heavily, heavily, heavily curated." He claims to have personally written operational reports that were later rewritten in official systems without his knowledge:

> "The only ultimate real currency that all this illusion is based off of is murder. And your most capable murderers are the guys you must control their information space the most."

He cites his own experience from 2013, while doing targeting work in Asia, noticing correlations between who was being targeted for strikes and subsequent market movements across different time zones — Singapore, London, and New York.

## Counter-Arguments

Burlingame's analysis, while internally coherent, has several weaknesses that deserve scrutiny.

**First**, the argument relies heavily on personal testimony and anecdote. While his claimed background in both algorithmic trading and Special Forces is plausible, many of the specific claims — particularly about intelligence database manipulation and Hezbollah's financial functions — are presented without verifiable sourcing. The viewer is asked to trust the speaker's expertise rather than examine evidence.

**Second**, the thesis risks becoming unfalsifiable. If every geopolitical conflict, every social movement, and every news cycle can be attributed to financial manipulation, the framework absorbs all contrary evidence as further proof of its own validity. The claim that "woke" politics, climate discourse, and NGO activity are all funded by the same mechanism stretches the model to near-universal explanatory power — a hallmark of conspiracy thinking rather than analysis.

**Third**, the depiction of algorithmic trading overstates its coherence. While high-frequency trading is well-documented, the idea that a single integrated system orchestrates information flows, geopolitical events, and market movements requires a level of coordination that strains credibility. Competing financial institutions are, as Burlingame himself acknowledges, "at war with one another." The system he describes would require unprecedented cooperation among actors who are simultaneously trying to destroy each other.

**Fourth**, the claim that Trump is deliberately disrupting this system assumes a level of strategic intentionality that his own communication history does not consistently support. Chaotic messaging may be a personality trait rather than a calculated strategy, even if the market effects are real.

## Conclusion

Burlingame's core observation — that algorithmic trading depends on predictable information flows and that genuinely unpredictable messaging disrupts those flows — is technically sound and worth serious consideration. The statistics on algorithmic trading dominance are well-established. The insight that markets reward predictable volatility more than mere volatility is a genuine contribution to public understanding.

However, the analysis dramatically overreaches when it expands from this foundation into a comprehensive theory of global manipulation. The most valuable elements are specific and testable: the relationship between data broker architecture and information flow, the economics of chaos premiums at geographic choke points, and the mechanics of how curated information environments shape decision-making. The weakest elements are the sweeping attributions of intentionality and the tendency to fold every global event into a single explanatory framework.

The evidence for deliberate information manipulation for financial gain is stronger than the evidence that this manipulation constitutes a unified global system. The former is documented in academic literature on market manipulation, media effects, and regulatory capture. The latter remains an assertion that requires substantially more evidence than a single interview can provide.

## Implications

If even the narrow version of Burlingame's thesis is correct — that algorithmic trading profits from manufactured volatility — the implications are significant.

**For markets**: The rise of genuinely unpredictable political communication may represent a structural change in market microstructure. If algorithmic systems are calibrated for predictable volatility, sustained unpredictability could reduce the profitability of certain trading strategies and redirect capital toward different approaches. This is not necessarily bad for markets; it may simply be different.

**For regulation**: The relationship between information manipulation and market profit raises questions about whether current regulatory frameworks adequately address the weaponization of information flows. Securities law focuses on insider trading and market manipulation through direct mechanisms, but may not adequately cover the indirect manipulation of information environments to move markets.

**For democratic discourse**: The suggestion that emotional manipulation generates financial profit creates a perverse incentive structure where political polarization is literally profitable. Understanding this dynamic — even in its weaker, non-systemic form — may help citizens approach emotionally charged news with appropriate skepticism.

**For geopolitics**: The concept of "chaos premiums" at strategic choke points offers a useful analytical lens for understanding why certain conflicts persist despite having no obvious strategic rationale. If volatility itself is the product, then resolution is the enemy of profit — and the interests of financial extraction may actively work against diplomatic solutions.

The question is not whether information manipulation for financial gain occurs. It does. The question is whether it constitutes a coherent system or an emergent property of competing actors pursuing similar strategies within shared infrastructure. The distinction matters enormously for anyone seeking to understand — or change — how the modern world actually works.
